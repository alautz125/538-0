538–0

Spin a president from real 1980–2024 contenders.

It’s 2028. Spins build your candidate from the parts of real contenders. Win a state-by-state primary, spin a running mate, draw a random opposing ticket, spend your war chest, survive October, and watch the results come in. Win, and you can run again.

    How the simulation works

    Spins. Each spin draws a real candidate from 1980–2024. Claim one of their traits for an open slot. You get re-spins for the build (4 on Easy, 2 on Hard), one for your running mate and one for your opponent, whose ticket pairs a nominee and a running mate from the other party, with centrists more likely to come up.

    Year doesn't move the race: it's always the 2028 election (2032 if you run again), fought on today's state electorates. Instead, your Year gives 2028 the mood of that cycle. It sets the national environment, based on that cycle's popular vote, and decides which era's primary voters you face: Republican voters are at their most hawkish and Democratic voters most moderate on social issues in the 1980s and '90s, Democrats drift left over time, and Republicans turn less hawkish on foreign policy after 2016. The huge 1980s landslides are softened so they don't swamp the game.

    Economy brings that year's real numbers: GDP growth, inflation, unemployment and the Fed's interest rate (annual figures from the BEA, BLS and Federal Reserve). Growth helps; inflation, unemployment and high rates hurt. Whoever held the White House in your Year gets the credit or the blame, so a strong economy helps you only if your party was in power.

    Campaign focus is your choice once the build is done: economic issues, social issues or foreign policy. In the primary, it adds a bonus each contest if your stance on that issue is close to what that round's voters want, and a penalty if it isn't. In November, it pays off if you're closer to the center on that issue than your opponent, and economic or social focus also magnifies regional reactions to your views. Social and foreign policy carry bigger stakes, and voter interest in them by fall is less predictable.

    Ideology pulls in two directions. Primary voters reward matching the party base. The general electorate rewards staying near the middle, with foreign policy counting a bit less. Regions react differently too: social conservatism plays better in the South and rural Midwest and worse on the coasts, while economic conservatism plays better in the Sun Belt and Mountain West and worse in the Rust Belt.

    Occupation works like a résumé. A sitting or former president's job gives your candidate a major primary boost, but under the two-term limit that candidate can't run for re-election after winning. A sitting or former vice president's job gives a moderate boost, with no limit. Governors and senators come next, House members less, and commentators and activists the least. It also sets your starting war chest. Age costs you under 38 or over 75 in the primary, and over 72 in the general. Home state helps in its primary round and is worth 4 points in November; your running mate's is worth 2.

    Style is how your candidate runs, and every real candidate on the board carries one. Establishment candidates get +0.35 in every contest from Super Tuesday on as the party consolidates, $10M more from big donors, a slightly better shot at rivals’ endorsements and +0.3 in November. Populists get +0.35 in the first three contests, do better at straw polls, raise more small-dollar money, and in November gain 0.6 points in the Midwest and 0.3 in the South while losing 0.9 in the Northeast and West. Mavericks do best in contests that draw moderates and independents, like New Hampshire, but the party base is wary everywhere else (about −0.1 a contest on average), endorsements are a bit harder to land, and in November their crossover appeal is worth +0.6. Style also nudges your odds in the debates.

    The primary runs seven rounds: Iowa, New Hampshire, Nevada and South Carolina, Super Tuesday, then March, April and May–June contests. Democrats in the two 2028 modes follow a new calendar instead: South Carolina, Nevada, New Hampshire and New Mexico, Michigan and Virginia, Super Tuesday, late March and April, then May and June. Winning a round builds momentum and money, and the field narrows to 7, 5, 4, 3 and then 2. Delegate rules follow each party. Democrats award delegates proportionally to anyone above 15% in every contest. Republicans do the same early on, then switch to winner-take-all by state (from March in today’s rules, from Super Tuesday before 2012), so a front-runner can pull away fast. In historical Democratic races from 1984 to 2016, superdelegates also vote: they declare in waves, leaning toward establishment candidates early and the delegate leader later. Since 2018 they vote only on a second ballot. The first to a majority clinches (1,001 of 2,000 pledged delegates, more when superdelegates vote). If nobody clinches, the convention is contested: on the second ballot, the delegates held by candidates who dropped out can switch, and you bargain for them by offering the VP slot, adopting a rival’s platform (which can cost you in November) or paying off their campaign debt. Each offer shows its odds of being accepted, and your rival makes offers too. Before Super Tuesday (before Michigan and Virginia on the 2028 Democratic calendar), something happens on the trail: a viral stumble, a viral endorsement, a debate clash, outside attack ads, a big endorsement in play or campaign turmoil. Your style shapes the odds of each response, and it moves the next two contests. Before each contest you can buy ads there: early states are cheap ($3M a buy) and Super Tuesday and the big spring contests cost $12M–$15M. Positive ads are a sure +0.4 for the first buy, with less for each extra one. Attack ads on one of your top three rivals land 65% of the time (−0.7 for them, halved if they’re on the air in that contest too) and backfire otherwise (−0.3 for you), and a rival you’ve attacked is harder to win an endorsement from. Ad effects last only for that contest. After Iowa, before Nevada and South Carolina, and before March, you choose a move: go all in on the next round, attack your top rival, pivot to the center, fire up the base or save your money. Pivoting and firing up the base carry over to November.

    Fundraising. Before the primary, pick one or two money sources. Small-dollar grassroots money is modest but wins over primary voters and pays more after each win (especially for candidates with strong views); pairing it with big donors cuts that goodwill. Big donors raise the most but cost you with the base. A super PAC adds $35M once you’re nominated, with a 1-in-4 chance its ads backfire in October. Self-funding is for business candidates and wins a little independence credit in November. Ask steadily or aggressively (more money, wider swings), then see where your haul ranks against the field. The top three get momentum into Iowa and New Hampshire; the bottom third start behind.

    Before the votes: a straw poll and a debate. After fundraising, party activists hold a straw poll. Go all in ($8M), make a solid effort ($3M) or skip it. Winning is worth +0.5 in the first two contests and $5M; second, third and a bottom-third finish count too, and whoever wins gets a boost. Then comes the first primary debate, with two moments: a question on your campaign focus (lay out your plan, go after the front-runner or play to the base) and an attack from a rival (hit back, laugh it off or rise above it). Each answer shows its odds. The result lifts or drags you in the first three contests, and a landed blow on the front-runner hurts them there too.

    Your endorser. After the build, pick one of four big names to back you: three from your party and one crossover from the other. Their help depends on geography (a boost in their home state’s primary round, then +1.5 in their state and +0.5 across their region in November), ideology (fitting your party’s base helps in the primary; being near the center helps in November) and résumé (former presidents and governors carry the most weight). A crossover endorser costs you with primary voters but helps in November. Whoever you pick can’t run against you, as a primary rival, an opponent or a third-party spoiler.

    Endorsements. Twice per primary, when a notable rival drops out, you can ask for their endorsement. It’s easier to land from someone close to you ideologically, harder if you attacked them, and a rival from the other wing of the party helps more in November.

    The running-mate call. Lose the primary and you may still make the ticket. Your chance starts at 5% and grows with a strong primary showing (especially second place), a big leftover war chest and a strong résumé. Coming from a different region than the nominee helps, and sharing their home state hurts. Ideologically, the sweet spot is close enough to the nominee to avoid clashing but different enough to broaden the ticket; balancing an extreme nominee toward the center helps too. If you get the call, you join the nominee’s ticket for the fall: you run the campaign, take the vice-presidential debate (worth half as much as the top-of-ticket debate) and can win the Second in command badge.

    The convention follows your running-mate pick. After a bruising primary, giving your top rival a prime-time slot can unite the party for a real bounce, though it can backfire if you two are far apart or you attacked them. You can also stay on your own message or go big with a star-studded show.

    Running-mate trouble. Every ticket carries a hidden risk of trouble with the running mate, about 1 in 10 and higher for business and media figures, whose records are harder to vet. The trouble is always on your campaign’s side (a rushed vetting, a staff feud or a ballot-paperwork mix-up), never a claim about the real person. If it hits in the fall, you can stand by them (a coin flip between a small and a big hit) or replace them with a new spin for a certain, smaller cost.

    Other ways to count. The results show how your race would have gone under a national popular vote, proportional electoral votes, and the congressional district method.

    Third parties. About a third of the time, a third-party candidate such as Ross Perot, Ralph Nader or Gary Johnson enters the race, likelier when a nominee is far from the center. Green and Libertarian candidates pull mostly from one party; independents pull from whichever nominee is more extreme.

    Polling. In the primary, public polls of the next contest are off by about 3 to 4 points per candidate; for a small fee you can commission your own polls of any contest (±1), updated after every round until it votes. In the fall, every odds figure on the campaign map is an estimate from the polls. Public state polls are off by about 3 points in battlegrounds and more elsewhere, and they share a systematic miss that also shows up in the national tracking polls. Paying for your own poll in a state ($1M to $6M by size) gives a ±1 read there with almost none of that miss, refreshed after the campaign push, the debate and October. After the election you see how far off each kind of poll was.

    Tracking polls run weekly from Labor Day to Election Day and react to your convention, campaign spending, the debate and October. They carry sampling error, and the result can still beat or miss them.

    The war chest starts with your résumé, grows with primary wins, and gets $40M from the party once you're nominated. In the campaign you get 4 free rallies (1.5 points in a state, 0.75 for a second) and can buy TV ads in $8M chunks, as many as you can afford (1 point for the first buy in a state, then diminishing returns) or a national ad blitz ($30M for 0.7 points everywhere). Ads can be positive or attack ads. An attack ad lands 65% of the time for 2.2 times a positive ad’s punch and backfires the rest of the time (−0.8 times), and it does half as much in a state where your opponent is also rallying or advertising. Attack buys share diminishing returns with positive buys in the same state. The other side spends its own money in the closest states, goes negative where it trails, and its plan is shown so you can respond; its attack ads do half as much where you’re campaigning. Field offices cost $4M, one per state: each adds 0.5 points and cuts that state’s share of polling error, which protects a lead but won’t rescue a state you trail. The other side opens a few in big states it leads narrowly. The debate is three questions: the economy, social issues and foreign policy. For each, you attack, lay out your plan, or pivot to your campaign focus; your traits and your opponent’s set the odds, and the snap poll moves the race by up to 2.5 points. October brings one random surprise; your traits change the odds that a risky response works.

    Election night starts each state at its 2024 partisan lean, applies both tickets' strengths, the national mood, regional fit, home states, rallies, ads and the October surprise, then squeezes big national swings to reflect today’s polarized electorate and runs 10,000 simulations with national, regional and state-level error. You step through the night by Eastern poll-closing time: blowouts are called right away, close states hours later. Uncalled states are shaded by who leads the count so far, and early leads can mislead: states that count mail ballots late, like Pennsylvania, Michigan and Wisconsin, lean Republican early and shift toward Democrats, while states that report early votes first, like Arizona, North Carolina and Ohio, do the reverse. The shift is biggest in 2020, smaller today and small before 2020. It never changes who wins. Swing states are any your side wins between 15% and 85% of the time. A 269–269 tie goes to the House, where Republicans hold most state delegations.

    Congress. House and Senate results ride your coattails, following your popular-vote margin. Holding both chambers makes your first-term priorities likelier to pass; facing a hostile Congress makes them harder. Losing the House at the midterms makes your last priority harder still.

    Recounts. If the presidency hinges on a state decided by less than half a point, election night ends without a call and you choose how to fight the recount: in court, with a hand count, or by conceding.

    Governing and re-election. Win, and you can run again. First you govern. You pick a Treasury, State and Defense secretary from three options each; their stance and résumé raise or lower the odds of related priorities, experienced picks steady your crisis response, and a Senate run by the other party may reject nominees far from the center. Then pick three priorities in order, such as tax cuts, health coverage, a climate bill or a military intervention. Your ideology sets each one’s odds, and a landslide win gives you a mandate. The first two come before the midterms, where the party in power usually loses seats; a wave election makes your third priority harder. In year 3, a random crisis strikes, such as a financial crash, a pandemic, an invasion of an ally or a scandal at a federal agency, and your response (with odds set by your traits) goes into the record too. The results become your record, which replaces the national mood and economy. You then run as the sitting president, four years older, with the same running mate, against a new challenger. Incumbents raise more: your war chest is about $175M on Easy and $150M on Hard, higher with a strong approval rating and lower with a weak one. Challengers to a sitting president raise $125M to $175M, more with a bigger résumé.

    Modes and difficulty

    Hypothetical 2028 is the main game: today’s state electorates, with your Year setting the mood. Realistic 2028 has you pick one of 20 real 2028 contenders, from JD Vance and Gavin Newsom to Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and Glenn Youngkin, with their traits locked in. You spin only for the national mood and the economy. Republicans hold the White House, so a past year’s mood carries over as a mood toward the party in power, and the GOP gets the credit or blame for the economy. You face the rest of your party’s real field, with everyone’s starting odds set by the RealClearPolling national primary averages (July 19 to Sept. 28, 2026), scaled to the 10-candidate field; endorsers, running mates and cabinet picks are today’s party figures; and your opponent is drawn from the other party’s field, weighted by their primary odds. What-if drops your candidate into a real year from 1980 to 2024: that year’s mood and economy, its real primary field, the real opposing ticket and its real third-party run, all on that year’s state-by-state map. Scenario puts you in the shoes of a real candidate (Ted Kennedy in 1980, Gary Hart in 1984, Hillary Clinton in 2008, Ted Cruz in 2016 and more), with their real traits and their real primary field. Your starting nomination odds are calibrated to roughly where they stood in national primary polls when primary season began, and cut further if you face a sitting or former president or vice president, who also gains strength from Super Tuesday on as the establishment consolidates; your endorser, focus, fundraising and moves take it from there. Kamala Harris’s 2024 scenario starts after Biden steps aside. Endorsers, running mates and cabinet picks come from people active around that year, super PACs only exist from 2008 on, and the general election starts from roughly where the real race stood in June polling, adjusted for how your ticket differs from the real nominee’s. Electoral votes follow the census apportionment in effect that year, and Maine and Nebraska split their votes by district the way they did then. Afterward you see how you did compared with what actually happened. Win, and you can run again four years later on the next real map against that year’s real opposing ticket (through 2020).

    Easy gives 4 re-spins, a primary boost, +0.8 nationally in November, 20% more money and better odds for first-term priorities. Hard gives 2 re-spins and flips each of those against you, with 15% less money.

    Your candidate. Once your build is done, the bar pinned at the top shows your candidate at a glance; tap it for the full card, your running mate, your opponent and your run so far.

    Tutorial tips. Turn them on at the top of the build screen or at the bottom of the page. A short tip appears the first time you reach each stage, and the control it refers to is outlined in gold. Tap Got it to dismiss a tip, or Turn off tips to stop them.

    Saving. Your run is saved on this device after every step. If you close the page, you can pick up where you left off. Every president you elect goes into the Hall of presidents, along with stats on how your runs have gone.
